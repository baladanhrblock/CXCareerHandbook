Row 1 — Communication Definition: How well the designer articulates ideas and rationale to their team and stakeholders.

Associate: Presents work clearly to the immediate team and explains what they made. Needs support framing the "why" for stakeholders.
Mid: Explains rationale, not just output. Handles questions in review without a leader stepping in.
Senior: Tailors the story to the audience, adapting depth and framing for design, product, and business partners. Written artifacts stand on their own.
Lead: Drives alignment in rooms they don't own. Turns disagreement into a decision the team can act on.
Principal: Sets how design communicates across the org and represents design's point of view to executives.

Row 2 — Strategic Thinking Definition: How well the designer connects their daily work to broader business objectives and customer problems.

Associate: Can state which customer problem their task serves when asked.
Mid: Connects feature decisions to stated goals and metrics without prompting; asks why a request exists.
Senior: Reframes requests around the underlying customer and business problem, and pushes back on solutions that miss it.
Lead: Shapes the roadmap for their area — decides what design should work on, not just how.
Principal: Influences business strategy with experience insight and names opportunities the org hadn't seen.

Row 3 — Ambiguity Definition: How well the designer navigates unclear problems and adapts to shifting constraints.

Associate: Asks for clarification early rather than stalling. Works well when the brief is defined.
Mid: Makes progress on loosely defined work by proposing a starting point and testing it.
Senior: Creates structure where there was none — frames the problem, sequences the unknowns, keeps the team moving.
Lead: Holds direction steady when constraints shift; decides what to protect and what to trade.
Principal: Takes on the org's most undefined problems and returns with a defensible path.

Row 4 — Collaboration Definition: How effectively the designer partners across disciplines and within their own team to achieve shared goals.

Associate: Participates actively, shares work early, and responds to feedback without defensiveness.
Mid: Partners directly with engineering and product on their scope; anticipates what partners need and when.
Senior: Builds trust across functions and brings partners in at the right moment rather than presenting finished work.
Lead: Establishes how design and its partner teams work together, and resolves friction between functions.
Principal: Builds cross-org relationships that unblock design work before it gets blocked.

Row 5 — Impact Definition: How much the designer contributes to the product and the organization.

Associate: Delivers assigned work that ships. Contribution is visible at the task level.
Mid: Owns a feature end to end; their work measurably improves the experience.
Senior: Moves outcomes for a product area; their decisions show up in customer and business metrics.
Lead: Multiplies impact through others — the team ships better because of how they've set it up.
Principal: Changes what the org is able to do; impact compounds beyond any single product.

Row 6 — Autonomy Definition: How independently the designer manages their own work and drives projects forward.

Associate: Manages assigned tasks and flags blockers. Checks in frequently by design.
Mid: Runs their own workstream, sets their own sequence, and hits commitments without oversight.
Senior: Operates with direction rather than instruction; leaders hear about the problem and the fix at the same time.
Lead: Manages a portfolio of work and other people's dependencies, and creates the clarity that lets others be autonomous.
Principal: Sets their own agenda in service of org goals and defines the work worth doing.

Row 7 — Leadership Definition: How well the designer supports peers and elevates the team's capabilities.

Associate: Supports peers informally — shares what they learn and contributes to team rituals.
Mid: Onboards and mentors newer designers; improves the team's practices in small, visible ways.
Senior: Actively raises the bar around them through critique, mentorship, and modeling craft standards.
Lead: Grows designers deliberately and may step into an interim Lead/POC role. Accountable for team capability, not just output.
Principal: Develops leaders and shapes the design org's culture and standards.
